Press-on forklift tires: sizes, types and when to replace

How to read a press-on forklift tire size, pick between smooth, traction, non-marking and polyurethane, spot when they're worn out, and get them replaced on site.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated November 12, 2026About 6 minutes

If your forklift runs inside a warehouse on smooth concrete, it's almost certainly sitting on press-on tires. They're the low, solid black (or gray) tires bonded to a steel band. No air, no flats, not much to think about, until the day the truck starts bouncing, leaning or chewing up your floor.

Here's what to know before you call for quotes.

What a press-on tire is

A press-on tire is solid rubber or polyurethane molded onto a steel band. A hydraulic press pushes it onto the forklift's wheel, and it stays put because the fit is so tight. That's why it's called press-on, and why you also hear them called cushion tires. Our tire vs. wheel vs. rim guide has a diagram if you want the details.

How to read the size

Press-on sizes are three numbers, all in inches: outside diameter x width x inside diameter.

A 21x7x15 is 21 inches tall, 7 inches wide, and fits a 15-inch wheel. Easy. It's usually molded on the sidewall or stamped on the band. Fractions matter: an 18x6x12-1/8 (written 18x6x12.125) is not the same as anything with a 12.

Pick the right type

  • Standard rubber, smooth. The everyday tire for dry concrete. Long life, good stability.
  • Traction tread. Grooved for wet floors, ramps and dock plates.
  • Non-marking. Gray or white rubber that won't leave black streaks. Food plants, retail floors, clean rooms. They usually wear a bit faster.
  • Polyurethane. Mostly on pallet jacks, reach trucks and narrow-aisle trucks. Carries heavy loads on a small tire and rolls easily, but doesn't like heat or rough floors.
  • Wide or high-capacity. Extra width for more stability and load. Make sure it clears the frame.

When to replace them

Press-on tires don't go flat, so they get ignored. Here's what to watch:

  • The wear line. Many press-on tires have a line molded into the sidewall. When the rubber wears down to it, replace the tire. On many tires it's at the top of the lettering.
  • Chunking. Pieces missing from the edges or tread.
  • Flat spots or out-of-round wear. The truck rides rough or bounces.
  • Separation. Rubber pulling away from the steel band. Replace it now.
  • Cracks, especially between the rubber and the band.
  • Uneven pairs. If one drive tire is a lot smaller than the other, the truck leans and steers funny.

Worn tires aren't just a ride issue. A forklift sitting lower than it should affects stability and how high it lifts its load, and it shakes the mast, battery and operator all day long.

I'll confess one. When I was 21, I worked in a warehouse that had brand-new forklifts on solid press-on tires. We had a mobile loading dock, the kind you roll around and back up to the back of a semi, and its deck was steel grating covered in little raised nibs so nobody slid down it. I thought it'd be fun to lock up the wheels on that grating and see what happened. What happened is I chewed those brand-new press-on tires to pieces. I feel pretty bad about it now. It was fun at 21.

But it taught me how fast rough steel eats a press-on tire. If your trucks run over dock plates and grating all day, start watching for chunking a lot sooner than you'd think.

Replace in pairs

Always replace both tires on the same axle. A new tire next to a worn one makes the truck lean and puts uneven load on the drive train.

How replacement works

You have two easy options:

  1. Mobile press truck. A service truck with a press on board comes to your building, pulls the wheels, presses on new tires, and you're back in business, often in an hour or two per truck.
  2. Wheel swap. The service brings wheels with new tires already pressed on and takes your old ones back.

Either way, ask for the tire, pressing labor and trip charge together so you can compare apples to apples. Prices vary a lot by size, compound and how many trucks you're doing at once, so get two or three quotes. Search for a forklift tire service near you; most will quote all three over the phone.

Common questions

Can I press on forklift tires myself?

Not without a press made for it. It takes many tons of force and the right adapters.

How long do press-on tires last?

It depends on hours, load, floors and how hard drivers turn. Watch the wear line instead of the calendar.

What's the difference between press-on and cushion tires?

Nothing. They're two names for the same tire.

Can I put pneumatic tires on a cushion forklift?

No. Cushion trucks and pneumatic trucks are built on different frames, wheels and axles.

What does 21x7x15 mean on a forklift tire?

21 inches tall, 7 inches wide, and it fits a 15-inch steel wheel.
