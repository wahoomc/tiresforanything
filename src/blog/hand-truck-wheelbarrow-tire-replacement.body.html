Wagon, wheelbarrow and hand truck tires: flat-free vs. air

Replace a hand truck, wagon or wheelbarrow tire the easy way: read the size, measure the axle and hub, and choose flat-free or air-filled.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated November 13, 2026About 5 minutes

There's always one. The hand truck in the garage, the wheelbarrow behind the shed, the wagon the kids hauled firewood in. It's flat again. And it was flat the last time you needed it too.

The good news: these are the easiest tires on this whole site to replace yourself. Most come already mounted on a wheel. You slide the old one off the axle, slide the new one on, and you're done. You just have to buy the right one, and that takes about two minutes with a tape measure.

Step 1: Read the tire size

Look on the sidewall. You'll usually see one of these:

  • 4.10/3.50-4. The most common hand truck tire. About 10 inches tall, on a 4-inch wheel. The two widths mean the tire fits either listing.
  • 4.80/4.00-8. A common wheelbarrow and garden cart tire. About 16 inches tall, on an 8-inch wheel.
  • 13x5.00-6. Another wheelbarrow and cart size. 13 inches tall, 5 wide, 6-inch wheel.
  • 10x2.75 or just 10 inch. Lots of wagons and some light hand trucks.

Can't read it? Measure the tire straight across, top to bottom. That's the diameter, and it gets you close.

Step 2: Measure the axle and hub

If you're buying a tire already on a wheel (and you should), the tire size is only half of it. You also need:

  1. Bore size. The hole in the middle that the axle goes through. Measure the axle itself. 5/8 inch is the most common on hand trucks. 3/4 inch and 1/2 inch are common too.
  2. Hub length. How wide the center of the wheel is, end to end. It decides how the wheel sits on the axle. Wheelbarrows are picky about this because the wheel has to fit between the two arms of the frame.
  3. Offset. Some wheels have the hub centered, some have it pushed to one side. Look at your old one.

A wheel that's a little too narrow can usually be fixed with spacers. A wheel that's too wide won't fit at all.

Most flat-free tires are polyurethane foam, molded solid. Our tire vs. wheel vs. rim guide explains the whole flat-proof family if you want the details.

My honest take: for a hand truck or a wheelbarrow that sits for weeks between jobs, flat-free wins. You'll never walk out to a flat again. For a garden cart that hauls heavy loads across soft ground, air still rolls easier.

Wheelbarrows and hand trucks usually sit for long stretches between jobs. And a lot of times, when you finally go grab one, the air tire is flat. Bearings matter more than you think

Cheap wheels use a plastic bushing in the center. It works, but it wears and squeaks. Better wheels use roller or ball bearings that roll easier under load. If you move heavy stuff often, it's worth a few extra dollars.

Want to keep your air tires?

If you'd rather stay with air, add a preventive sealant or a thicker tube. Our leak stops guide covers the options for stopping thorn flats.

Common questions

What does 4.10/3.50-4 mean?

A tire that fits a 4-inch wheel and can be listed as either 4.10 or 3.50 wide. It's the most common hand truck size.

Can I replace just the tire, not the wheel?

Yes, but on small wheels it's usually a fight. Buying the tire already mounted is easier and often barely costs more.

What size axle does a hand truck have?

5/8 inch is the most common. Measure yours to be sure.

Are flat-free tires worth it?

For tools that sit for a while and need to work when you grab them, yes.
