Why is my tire pressure light on when it's cold?

Cold mornings drop tire pressure about 1 psi per 10°F and trip the TPMS light. What it means, how to fix it in five minutes, and when it's something else.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated November 13, 2026About 4 minutes

First cold morning of the year, you start the car and there it is: that yellow horseshoe with the exclamation point. Then by lunch it's gone. Nothing's wrong with the car. Something's wrong with the air.

My wife calls it the excited horseshoe. One look at it and you'll see why.

I have to remind her that most of the time, her tires are fine. On a cool fall morning, the side of the car sitting in the shade stays cold while the sun warms up the tires on the other side. The cold side reads a few pounds lower, and sometimes that's just enough to make the sensors think one side of the car has a problem. Top them off and the light goes away.

Why cold air sets it off

Air shrinks when it gets cold. A tire set at 35 psi on a 70-degree September afternoon can be sitting around 30 psi on a 20-degree November morning, without a single leak. That's the rule of thumb tire people use: about 1 psi per 10 degrees.

Your tire pressure monitoring system (TPMS) watches for low tires. Most systems light up when a tire gets to roughly 25% below the recommended pressure. If your tires were already a few pounds low going into fall, that first big cold snap pushes them over the line.

Why the light goes off later

Driving heats the tires, and the afternoon warms things up. The pressure climbs back above the warning point and the light shuts off. That doesn't mean the problem fixed itself. It means you're running low every morning until you add air.

Fix it in five minutes

  1. Check the tires cold. Before you drive, or after the car has sat a few hours. Warm tires read high.
  2. Find the right number. It's on the sticker inside the driver's door jamb. The number on the tire's sidewall is the maximum, not what you should run.
  3. Fill all four. And check the spare while you're down there.
  4. Drive a few miles. Most lights reset on their own once every tire is back up. Some cars have a reset button or a menu setting; your owner's manual will say.

A cheap gauge and a little inflator in the garage make this a two-minute job instead of a trip to the gas station in the cold.

When it isn't just the cold

  • One tire keeps going low. That's a leak. Could be a nail, a leaky valve stem, or the bead at the rim. Our leak stops guide covers your options.
  • The light flashes for about a minute, then stays on. On most cars that means a problem with the system itself, often a sensor battery wearing out. Sensors last years, not forever.
  • The light came on after new tires or a rotation. Some systems need to relearn where each sensor is. The shop can do that.

Common questions

Is it safe to drive with the tire pressure light on?

Short distance to air, yes. But a tire 25% low wears faster, handles worse and heats up. Don't ignore it for weeks.

Should I fill to the number on the tire?

No. That's the tire's max. Use the door jamb sticker.

How much air do I add in winter?

Fill to the door sticker number with the tires cold. Don't overfill to make up for the cold; checking more often works better.

Why is only one tire low?

Cold affects all four evenly. One low tire usually means a slow leak.
