Tire basics · Definitions

Tire vs. wheel vs. rim, and the other words nobody explains

Plus casters, solid, press-on, foam-filled and run-flat tires (including the kind inside a Humvee), and the tractor tires with beet juice in them. Plain English, no shop-counter jargon.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated October 25, 2026About 9 minutes

Our site's tagline is "the right tire for anything that rolls." Which sounds great right up until you realize half the people looking for a tire aren't sure that's the word they need. Maybe it's the wheel. Maybe it's the rim. Maybe the thing under the office chair is a caster, and the thing under the forklift is a press-on, and the thing under the skid steer is full of foam.

So before we write a single buying guide, here's the vocabulary. I'll keep it short where it can be short and go a little deeper where getting it wrong costs you money or a trip back to the store.

The quick answer
Tire
The rubber (or urethane) part that touches the ground.
Rim
The outer edge of the wheel. It's where the tire sits and seals.
Wheel
The whole metal piece that bolts to the vehicle, rim included.
Wheel assembly
A tire that comes already mounted on its wheel, ready to bolt or slide on.
Caster
A small wheel mounted in a frame that bolts to the bottom of something, like a cart or a chair. Many swivel.

Everything else on this page is a type of tire built so it can't go flat, or a tire with something besides air inside it.

Tire

The tire is the part that touches the ground. On most things it's a rubber casing full of air, and the air is actually doing most of the work. It holds up the weight, soaks up bumps and lets the tread flatten out a little so it can grip.

That air-filled kind is called pneumatic. Some pneumatic tires hold air with an inner tube (bicycles, wheelbarrows, a lot of old stuff). Others are tubeless, meaning the tire seals right against the rim. Your car's tires are tubeless.

Then there's everything that isn't full of air. That's the back half of this page.

Wheel

The wheel is the round metal (or plastic, or composite) piece the tire mounts on. It bolts to the hub of the vehicle with lug nuts or rides on an axle. On a car it's usually one cast or stamped piece. On a big loader or an old military truck it can be three, four or five pieces held together with rings.

If you can unbolt it and roll it across the driveway without the tire, you're holding a wheel.

Rim

Technically, the rim is just the outer lip of the wheel. It has two jobs. It gives the tire's bead (the stiff edge of the tire) a bead seat to sit on, and it has a raised flange that keeps the tire from sliding off sideways. When a tire size says "15-inch," that's the rim diameter measured at the bead seat, not the outside edge of the flange.

In everyday talk, though, "rims" means wheels. Shiny aftermarket wheels especially. If you call a shop and ask for new rims, they'll know exactly what you mean, and nobody's going to correct you. I've stopped correcting my own kids on this one. It's a losing battle.

Here's where the difference actually matters. Big trucks, farm equipment, forklifts and construction machines often run multi-piece rims (also called split rims), where a locking ring holds the tire on. If one of those comes apart under pressure, it can kill someone. OSHA has a whole standard on servicing them (29 CFR 1910.177). If your tire sits on a rim with a lock ring, that's not a garage project. Get a quote from a shop that does it every day.

Tire and wheel assembly

A tire and wheel assembly is a tire that comes already mounted on its wheel, aired up and ready to bolt or slide on. You'll also see it called a mounted tire, a wheel-and-tire combo, or just an assembly. Instead of buying a tire, finding someone to mount it and hoping it fits, you buy the whole thing.

I'm a big fan of these for small stuff. Lawn mowers, hand trucks, wheelbarrows, trailers, golf carts and wheelchairs all have tires that are a real fight to mount by hand, and a shop may charge nearly as much to mount one as the tire costs. Buy the assembly and you skip all of that.

Where you'll see them most:

  • Hand trucks, wheelbarrows and garden carts. The wheel slides onto an axle. Often flat-free.
  • Riding mowers and zero-turns. Front and rear wheels, air or flat-free.
  • Utility, boat and camper trailers. Bolt-on, and a smart way to carry a spare.
  • Golf carts, ATVs and UTVs. Often sold as matched sets of four, especially for lift kits.
  • Wheelchairs and mobility scooters. Rear wheels and casters both come complete.

The catch is that you're matching more than the tire size. Check these against your old wheel before you buy:

  • Bolt pattern (trailers, golf carts, ATVs). The number of lug holes and how far apart they are, like 5 on 4.5 inches.
  • Axle or bore size (hand trucks, wheelbarrows, mowers). The hole the axle slides through, like 5/8 or 3/4 inch.
  • Hub length and offset. How wide the center of the wheel is and where it sits, so it fits between the frame or fork.
  • Load rating, especially on trailers. The assembly has to carry your loaded weight.

An assembly that looks right but has the wrong bolt pattern, bore or offset won't fit, or worse, will fit badly. When in doubt, take your old wheel with you or measure it twice.

Caster (or castor)

A caster is a wheel that comes in its own little frame so you can bolt it to the bottom of something. Office chairs, shopping carts, tool chests, hospital beds, the front wheels on most wheelchairs, that rolling cart in the garage that only goes left. All casters.

"Caster" is the usual American spelling. "Castor" is more common in the UK. Same thing.

The two main kinds:

  • Swivel casters spin all the way around on a bearing, so the cart can turn.
  • Rigid (or fixed) casters only roll straight ahead. Put two of each on a cart and it tracks straight but still steers, which is why most carts are built that way.

When you shop for casters, the numbers that matter are wheel diameter, load rating per caster, how it mounts (a flat top plate or a threaded stem), and what the wheel is made of. Hard wheels roll easier. Soft rubber or polyurethane wheels are quieter and kinder to floors. A wheel lock or brake is worth the extra couple bucks if anything on that cart is heavy.

Car people: "caster" is also an alignment angle on your front suspension. Completely different thing. English is fun.

The flat-proof family

Air is great until it leaves. On a passenger car, a flat is an annoying afternoon. On a forklift in a warehouse full of nails and pallet splinters, or a skid steer on a demolition job, it's lost production every single week. So people found ways to take the air out of the equation. The next five terms are all variations on that idea, and they get mixed up constantly.

Solid tire

A solid tire has no air in it at all. It's rubber (or polyurethane) the whole way through. It can't go flat, it can't blow out, and it lasts a long time. The trade-off is a harder ride and a lot more weight.

You'll run into three kinds:

  • Solid pneumatic tires, also called resilient tires. Confusing name, I know. They're solid rubber, but they're shaped to fit the same rim an air-filled tire would use. You see them on outdoor forklifts and some construction equipment. They're sized like regular tires, such as 6.50-10.
  • Press-on tires, which get their own section below.
  • Flat-free tires for hand trucks, wheelbarrows, mowers and carts. Most are molded polyurethane foam, and lots of them come already mounted on a wheel so you just slide it on the axle. These are the easiest of the bunch to buy online and put on yourself.

Press-on tire

A press-on tire is a band of solid rubber (or polyurethane) bonded to a steel ring. It gets pressed onto the wheel with a hydraulic press that pushes with tons of force, and it stays put because the fit is so tight. No air, no bolts, no lock ring.

These are the low-profile, smooth-looking tires on indoor forklifts, which is why they're also called cushion tires. They're built for smooth concrete floors. Take one out on gravel and you'll feel every rock.

Press-on sizes read differently from almost everything else. A 21x7x15 is 21 inches tall, 7 inches wide, and fits a 15-inch wheel. Three numbers, all in inches, all straightforward. You can check any size in our size decoder.

Can you put one on yourself? Not without a tire press, and most people don't have one sitting in the garage. Most shops will either press them on-site or swap your wheels. Our forklift tire guide walks through it, and the quote form will get you prices.

Foam-filled tire

Take a normal air-filled tire, drain the air, and pump in liquid polyurethane. It cures into a solid, spongy rubber core. That's a foam-filled tire. It looks exactly like a regular tire from the outside, rides closer to an air tire than a solid does, and it won't go flat no matter what you drive over.

You'll find them on skid steers, telehandlers, scissor lifts, boom lifts and mowers that live around scrap or brush. Two things to know before you do it. They get heavy, which is why aerial lifts sometimes run them on purpose as ballast. And once that foam is in there, the tire isn't coming back out. When the tread is gone, you usually replace the whole tire-and-fill together.

Foam filling takes a pump rig and the right fill material, so it's a dealer job. See the construction and skid steer guide or the lawn and garden guide for options.

Liquid-filled tire

This one surprises people. A lot of farm tractors have liquid in their rear tires on purpose. It's called liquid ballast. The extra weight sits as low as it can possibly go, which helps the tractor grip, pull and stay upright, especially with a loader full of dirt on the front.

The usual fill is about 75 percent, which works out to the valve stem sitting at the 12 o'clock position. The tire still has air in it above the liquid. The common fluids:

  • Calcium chloride is the old standard. Heavy and cheap, and it won't freeze in a normal winter. It is also corrosive, so a slow leak can eat a steel rim and valve stem.
  • Beet juice is real, and yes, I laughed the first time too. Sold as Rim Guard, it's a sugar-beet byproduct. It weighs about the same as calcium chloride (roughly 10.7 to 11 pounds a gallon versus 8.3 for water), stays liquid to around 35 below, and it doesn't rust your rims.
  • Windshield washer fluid and antifreeze mixes are lighter but don't freeze. Plain water is the cheapest and freezes solid, so it only works where winters are mild.

You might also hear "liquid-filled" about tire sealants like Slime, the green goop you squirt in a bike or mower tire to plug small punctures. Same idea at a smaller scale: a few ounces in a tire that's mostly air.

Ballasting big ag tires is normally a farm dealer job. Start with the farm and ag tire guide.

Run-flat tire

A run-flat is still an air-filled tire. The difference is what happens when the air leaves. Instead of folding up on the rim, it keeps carrying the vehicle far enough to get you somewhere safe. There are three ways to pull that off, and only one of them is what a soldier means when they say "run-flat."

  • Self-supporting run-flats are what you'll find on a lot of BMWs, Minis and other cars that come without a spare. The sidewalls are thick and stiff enough to hold the car up with zero air. Most are rated for about 50 miles at up to 50 mph, but check the maker's numbers for your tire. Two catches. The ride is firmer, and because the tire doesn't look flat, you need a working tire pressure warning light to even know you've lost air. Many shops won't repair one after it's been driven flat.
  • Self-sealing tires have a sticky layer inside that closes up nail and screw holes as they happen. Technically they aren't run-flats. They just try to keep the air from leaving in the first place.
  • Insert run-flats (also called support-ring or "solid" run-flats) put a hard ring of rubber or composite around the wheel, inside the tire. Normally it just sits there. When the tire goes flat, it collapses down onto the ring, and the vehicle rides on the ring instead of the rim. That's the setup on military trucks, armored SUVs and police vehicles, because it holds up under heavy loads where a stiff sidewall can't.

The Humvee is the classic example. Inside its 37x12.50R16.5 tire sits a thick rubber insert that does two jobs. It's the run-flat, and it's also the beadlock, pinching the tire's beads out against the wheel so the tire can't slip or come off at low pressure. The wheel itself is two halves bolted together, which is the only way to get a tire and that big insert onto it. How far you can drive on it depends on the insert, the load and the terrain, so go by the vehicle's technical manual.

Two-piece wheels can hurt you

Never loosen the bolts that hold the two halves of a Humvee-style wheel together while the tire has any air in it. Let all the air out first. The run-flat insert is also packed in under heavy compression, and it takes the right tools to take it apart and put it back together. If you've never done one, have a shop that works on military vehicles handle it, or at least follow the technical manual to the letter.

Common questions

Are rims and wheels the same thing?

Not technically. The rim is the outer edge of the wheel, where the tire sits. But in everyday conversation people say "rims" when they mean wheels, and every tire shop understands that.

Can I put air in a solid tire?

No. Solid, press-on and molded flat-free tires don't have a valve or anywhere for air to go. If there's a valve stem, it's an air-filled tire, or it's a foam-filled or liquid-filled tire that started life as one.

Can I replace a press-on forklift tire myself?

Only if you have access to a tire press built for it. Press-on tires are held on by a very tight fit, and it takes a hydraulic press to push them on and off. Most owners have a mobile tire service do it on-site.

Is it worth foam-filling my mower or skid steer tires?

If you're fixing flats all the time, it can be. You trade a softer ride and lighter weight for never patching another tire. Keep in mind the added weight and that the tire and foam get replaced together when the tread wears out.

Do tractors really have beet juice in their tires?

Many do. A beet-juice product called Rim Guard is a popular liquid ballast because it's heavy, resists freezing and doesn't corrode rims the way calcium chloride can.

What kind of run-flat does a Humvee use?

An insert run-flat. A thick rubber ring sits on the wheel inside the tire and doubles as a beadlock. If the tire goes flat, the truck rides on the insert. The wheel is two halves bolted together, so always let all the air out before loosening those bolts.

Should I buy just the tire or the whole wheel assembly?

For small tires like hand trucks, mowers, wheelbarrows and trailers, the assembly is usually easier and often costs about the same once you count mounting. Just match the bolt pattern or axle size, hub length, offset and load rating to your old wheel.

Is it caster or castor?

Both. "Caster" is the common American spelling and "castor" is more common in British English. They mean the same wheel-in-a-frame part.
