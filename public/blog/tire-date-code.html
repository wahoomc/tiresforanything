How old is my tire? Reading the DOT date code

Find the four-digit date code on any tire, read it in seconds, and know how old is too old for cars, trailers, RVs and motorcycles.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated November 11, 2026About 5 minutes

There's a tire on your trailer, or your spare, or your motorcycle, that looks perfectly fine. Good tread, no cracks you can see. And it might be twelve years old.

Tires age whether they roll or not. The rubber dries out and loses its stretch from the inside, where you can't see it. The good news is every tire sold in the U.S. tells you exactly when it was made. You just have to know where to look.

Find the DOT code

Look on the sidewall for the letters DOT followed by a string of numbers and letters. That whole string is the Tire Identification Number. The part you want is the last four digits, often inside a little oval or box.

One catch. The full code is only required on one side of the tire. If you only see part of it, look at the other side. On a tire mounted on a car, that might mean the side facing in, so grab a flashlight and get down on the driveway.

Read the four digits

The first two digits are the week it was made. The last two are the year.

CodeMeans
2319Week 23 of 2019 (early June 2019)
0124Week 1 of 2024 (first week of January 2024)
5221Week 52 of 2021 (last week of December 2021)

That's it. The DOT age checker on our trailer tire page will do the math for you, too. If the date is only three digits, the tire was made before 2000. That's all you need to know. It's too old. Replace it.

How old is too old?

There isn't one federal rule, so here's what you'll hear:

  • Many vehicle makers say replace tires at 6 years from the date of manufacture, no matter how they look.
  • Many tire makers say have them inspected every year after 5 years, and never run them past 10 years.
  • Trailer and RV tires usually need replacing sooner, often around 3 to 5 years, because they sit, carry heavy loads and bake in the sun.

Where it matters most is the tires that steer and the tires that carry people. Steer tires are important. Bus tires are important. I drove charter buses on a CDL, and you don't gamble with either one.

I'll be honest about the other end of it, too. The Army, and plenty of vintage Army vehicles in private hands, roll on tires that are probably three and four times as old as they should be. That's not a recommendation. Always respect the date code and the manufacturer's recommendations. On anything that runs highway speeds with your family in it, I wouldn't push past six years. On a trailer, earlier.

Don't forget these tires

  • The spare. It's often the oldest tire you own and the one you trust least when you need it.
  • Trailers and campers that sit most of the year. Our trailer tire guide covers why they age out first.
  • Motorcycles that get ridden a few hundred miles a season. A bike tire can age out long before it wears out, and you only have two of them.
  • New tires. A tire can sit in a warehouse for a while before you buy it. It's fair to check the date before it's mounted. A tire a year or so old is normal.

Signs a tire has aged out

Small cracks in the sidewall or between the tread blocks, rubber that feels hard, bulges, or a tire that keeps losing air for no reason. If you see any of that, don't wait for the date.

Common questions

Where is the date on a tire?

In the last four digits of the DOT code on the sidewall. It may be on the inside-facing side only.

What does 3220 mean on a tire?

It was made in week 32 of 2020, which is early August.

Can a tire with good tread be too old?

Yes. Rubber breaks down with age even with full tread.

How old is too old for a tire?

Many vehicle makers say replace at 6 years from manufacture, many tire makers say never past 10, and trailer tires often need replacing at 3 to 5 years.

Are new tires from the store ever old?

Sometimes they've been in storage a while. Ask to see the date before you buy.
