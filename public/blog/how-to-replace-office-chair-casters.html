How to replace office chair casters, and pick the right casters for carts and shelves

Squeaky, wobbly or floor-scratching casters are a ten-minute fix. Here's how to swap office chair wheels, and how to pick casters for utility carts, rolling shelves and tool chests that actually hold up.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated October 25, 2026About 7 minutes

Casters are the tires nobody thinks about. They're under your office chair, the utility cart in the garage, the wire shelf in the pantry, the big red tool chest. And when one starts dragging, squealing or leaving gray streaks on the hardwood, most people just live with it.

You don't have to. Replacing casters is about as easy as a repair gets, and new ones usually cost less than a pizza. The only real trick is buying the right ones the first time.

Replacing office chair casters

Almost every office and gaming chair uses the same kind of caster: a wheel with a short metal stem on top that pushes up into the chair's base. There's a little spring ring, called a grip ring, around the stem that holds it in. No screws, no tools required most of the time.

  1. Flip the chair over. Lay it on its side or upside down on a towel so you don't scratch anything.
  2. Pull the old caster straight out. Grab the wheel and pull hard, wiggling it side to side. If it won't budge, slide a flathead screwdriver between the wheel and the base and pry gently. Some people wrap a towel around the wheel for grip.
  3. Measure the stem. Most are 7/16 inch thick and 7/8 inch long (also listed as 11 x 22 mm). Some chairs, especially flat-pack ones, use a 10 mm stem. A ruler is fine, but measure, don't guess.
  4. Push the new caster in. Line it up and push until you feel it snap past the grip ring. If it's tight, set the chair upright and sit down. Your weight will seat it. A rubber mallet works too.
  5. Do all five. Mixing old and new wheels makes the chair roll unevenly.

Casters for hardwood vs. carpet

This is the part most people get wrong. The cheap hard plastic wheels that come on most chairs are made for carpet. On hardwood, tile or laminate, they skid, rattle, and grind dirt into the floor until it's scratched up.

  • Hard floors: soft polyurethane or rubber wheels. The "rollerblade style" ones are popular for a reason. They're quiet, they roll smooth, and plenty of people find they can get rid of the chair mat entirely.
  • Carpet: hard nylon wheels roll easier. Soft wheels sink in and drag on thick carpet.
  • Bigger wheels, like 3 inch instead of 2 inch, roll over cords, rugs and thresholds more easily.

Plate, stem or threaded?

Outside of office chairs, casters attach a few different ways. Look at your old one:

  • Top plate. A flat metal plate with four bolt holes. Used on utility carts, workbenches, tool chests and most heavy stuff. Measure the plate and the distance between the holes, center to center.
  • Threaded stem. A bolt sticking up out of the caster that screws into the cart or shelf. Common on wire shelving and some carts. Match the thread, like 1/2-13 or 3/8-16. Take the old one to the hardware store if you're not sure.
  • Grip ring stem. The office chair style. Pushes in and snaps.
  • Expanding stem. A rubber sleeve that expands inside a round tube. Used on tube-frame carts, stools and walkers. Match the inside of the tube.

How much weight they carry

Every caster has a load rating, and this is where cheap ones fail. Here's the rule I'd go by: add up the heaviest thing the cart will ever hold, plus the cart itself, then divide by three. Not four.

Why three? Floors aren't perfectly flat. Roll a loaded cart across a garage floor and at any given moment one caster is barely touching, so the other three carry the whole load. A tool chest full of sockets and wrenches gets heavy fast, too. Give yourself room.

Carts, shelves and tool chests

  • Utility and service carts: 3 to 4 inch plate casters. Two swivel with brakes and two rigid is the classic setup; it tracks straight and still steers.
  • Rolling wire shelving: casters made for your shelf brand's posts, or a threaded stem that matches. Lock the brakes once it's where you want it.
  • Tool chests and workbenches: 5 x 2 inch plate casters are the common size for rolling tool chests. Get polyurethane on a steel hub, with brakes on at least two.
  • Heavy shop carts and dollies: 5 to 6 inch heavy-duty casters roll over cracks and cords without dumping your load.
  • Furniture: low-profile casters so the bed or dresser doesn't sit up too high.

Common questions

Are office chair casters universal?

Most are. The majority of office and gaming chairs use a 7/16 x 7/8 inch grip ring stem, also listed as 11 x 22 mm. Some use 10 mm stems, so pull one and measure before you buy.

How do I get a stuck office chair wheel out?

Pull straight out while wiggling the wheel. If it won't come, pry gently between the wheel and the chair base with a flathead screwdriver.

What casters won't scratch hardwood floors?

Soft polyurethane or rubber wheels, often sold as rollerblade-style chair casters. Hard plastic wheels are made for carpet.

How do I figure out what load rating I need?

Add the cart and its heaviest load together, then divide by three. Each caster should be rated for at least that much.

Should all four casters swivel?

Usually not. Two swivel and two rigid is easier to push straight. All four swivel is best for tight spaces where you need to move sideways.
