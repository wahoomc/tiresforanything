Tire basics · Balancing

Balance beads, stick-on or clip-on weights? What to use and when it matters

Beads are great in a motorcycle. But what about your car, your pickup, a semi or a lawn mower? Here's when balancing matters, when it doesn't, and which method fits which wheel.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated October 25, 2026About 8 minutes

No tire is perfectly round, and no tire weighs exactly the same all the way around. The wheel isn't perfect either. The valve stem alone puts a little extra weight on one side. At 10 miles an hour none of that matters. At 65, a heavy spot the weight of a couple of quarters is hammering the road hundreds of times a minute, and you feel it in the steering wheel, the seat, or the floorboards.

Balancing just means adding a small amount of weight in the right place so the whole tire and wheel spins smooth. There are three common ways to do it: beads inside the tire, weights stuck to the wheel, or weights clipped onto the rim's edge. Each one fits some machines better than others.

The quick answer
Balance beads
Tiny beads poured inside the tire that move to the light spot as it spins. Great for motorcycles, semis, RVs and big off-road truck tires.
Stick-on weights
Adhesive weights stuck inside the wheel barrel. The usual choice for aluminum car wheels and cast motorcycle wheels.
Clip-on weights
Weights that clamp onto the rim's outer lip. Standard for steel wheels, truck wheels and many factory alloys.
Skip it
Anything that rarely tops 25 to 30 mph (mowers, forklifts, tractors, wheelbarrows) usually doesn't get balanced at all.

When does balancing actually matter?

Speed is the big one. The force from an out-of-balance tire climbs with the square of speed, so going twice as fast makes the shake about four times worse. That's why a little imbalance is invisible around town and suddenly shows up between 50 and 70 mph.

Weight matters too. A heavy truck tire that's off by a few ounces can shake a whole steering axle, and over thousands of miles it wears the tread unevenly and beats up shocks, bearings and tie rods.

So here's a simple way to think about it:

  • Always balance anything that runs highway speeds. Cars, pickups, SUVs, motorcycles, RVs, semis, and yes, trailers. Trailer tires that run at 65 on the interstate deserve a balance just as much as the truck pulling them.
  • Sometimes balance ATVs, UTVs and lifted golf carts. If it shakes at the speeds you drive, balance it. If not, a lot of owners skip it.
  • Usually skip it on lawn mowers, forklifts, farm tractors, skid steers, wheelbarrows and hand trucks. They don't go fast enough for it to matter, and solid or foam-filled tires can't really be balanced anyway.
  • Aircraft are their own world. Wheel and tire balance is handled per the aircraft maintenance manual, usually by your A&P mechanic. A nose wheel that shimmies on the takeoff roll is worth mentioning to them.

Signs you need a balance: a shake in the steering wheel at highway speed (usually a front tire), a buzz in the seat or floor (usually a rear tire), or scalloped, cupped wear on the tread. If you just got new tires and it shakes, take it back. That's on the shop.

Static vs. dynamic balance, in plain English

Static balance fixes the heavy spot going up and down. Picture the wheel on a free-spinning axle: the heavy spot always rolls to the bottom. Add weight opposite it until the wheel stops wherever you leave it. That's how a lot of motorcycle riders balance in the garage with a simple stand.

Dynamic balance also fixes wobble side to side. A wide tire can be heavy on the inside edge in one spot and the outside edge in another, and it'll shimmy even if it passes a static test. A spin balancer at the shop measures both and tells the tech exactly how much weight goes on the inside and outside of the wheel.

Narrow tires like motorcycle and bicycle tires mostly need static balance. Car and truck tires need dynamic. If a car still shakes after a normal balance, ask for a road force balance. That machine presses a roller against the tire like the road does and can find a stiff spot or an out-of-round tire that weights alone won't fix.

Balance beads

Balance beads are small ceramic or glass beads (some are coated steel) that go inside the tire. Counteract and Dyna Beads are two big names. As the tire spins, the beads spread around the inside and pile up opposite the heavy spot, balancing the tire on the fly. As the tread wears or picks up mud, they shift and keep balancing.

Where beads shine:

  • Motorcycles. Beads work great here, especially on touring and cruiser bikes. You can add them yourself through the valve stem, they don't fall off at 75 mph the way a stick-on weight can, and no spinning machine is needed.
  • Semi trucks. This is where beads got big. Lots of fleets run them in steer and drive tires, and many add them right when the tire is mounted.
  • RVs and motorhomes. Same idea as the semi. Big, heavy tires that are tough to balance with weights.
  • Big off-road truck tires. A 35- or 37-inch mud tire can need a pile of weight, and mud packing into it changes the balance anyway. Beads keep up with it.

What about regular car tires? They can work, and bead makers sell kits for cars and light trucks. But this is where I'd pump the brakes a little. Car tires are wide and light, most shops still use weights, and a good shop balance on a quality tire is hard to beat. If your car shakes, a road force balance is a smarter first step than beads. I'd save beads for the bigger, heavier stuff and the bikes.

Bead tips before you buy:

  • Use the amount the bead maker lists for your tire size. Too few won't balance it.
  • Take off any old wheel weights first, or the beads will just be fighting them.
  • Use filtered valve cores. A stray bead in the valve can cause a slow leak or block your air chuck.
  • Keep moisture out. Wet air can make some beads clump. Dry shop air or nitrogen is best.
  • Don't mix beads with tire sealant like Slime. They turn into a gooey ball.
  • If you have tire pressure sensors, check the bead maker's guidance for your sensor type.
  • Beads need speed to spread out. A little vibration for the first block or two is normal.

Stick-on weights

Stick-on (adhesive) weights come in strips of small segments with double-sided tape on the back. They go on the inside of the wheel barrel, out of sight, so they won't scratch up a nice aluminum wheel. That's why they're the go-to for most alloy car wheels and cast motorcycle wheels.

The catch is the tape. The wheel has to be clean and dry, with no brake dust or tire lube, and they don't stick well in the cold. A stick-on that wasn't put on right will eventually fly off, and you'll notice the shake come back. Wiping the spot with alcohol first and pressing hard makes a big difference.

On a spoked motorcycle wheel, you'll usually see spoke weights instead. They clamp around a spoke near the rim.

Clip-on (clamp-on) weights

Clip-on weights, also called clamp-on or hammer-on weights, have a steel clip that grips the edge of the rim flange. A tech taps them on with a weight hammer. They hold great and they're cheap, which is why they're standard on steel wheels, trailer wheels and truck wheels.

Two details matter here. First, clips come in different shapes to fit different rim flanges, so the right clip for a Ford steel wheel isn't the right one for a semi wheel or an aluminum truck wheel. Second, on aluminum wheels you want coated weights made for alloy, because bare steel against aluminum can start corrosion.

If you're in one of the states that ban lead wheel weights (California, Washington, Minnesota, Illinois, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, Vermont and Maine), your shop will be using steel or zinc instead. Works the same. Just a little bigger for the same weight.

The red and yellow dots on new tires

Ever notice a little colored dot on the sidewall of a new tire? It's there to help with balancing.

  • A yellow dot marks the lightest spot on the tire. A good tech lines it up with the valve stem, since the valve adds a bit of weight to that side of the wheel. Less weight needed.
  • A red dot marks the tire's high spot, where it's slightly out of round. If the wheel has a mark for its low spot, the red dot goes there so the two cancel out. When a tire has both, the red dot wins.

Not every tire has them, and some brands use other marks. But if you're mounting your own motorcycle or trailer tires, lining up the dot is free and it can save you some weight.

Common questions

Do balance beads work in car tires?

They can, and kits are sold for cars. But most shops still use weights on passenger cars because the tires are wide and light, and a good spin balance works very well. Beads tend to make the most sense in motorcycles, semis, RVs and big off-road truck tires.

Are stick-on or clip-on weights better?

Neither is better across the board. Clip-ons hold best and are standard on steel wheels. Stick-ons are used on aluminum wheels where a clip would scratch the finish or won't fit the rim edge. The wheel decides.

Do trailer tires need to be balanced?

If the trailer runs highway speeds, yes. Unbalanced trailer tires wear faster and shake the trailer, even though you can't feel it from the driver's seat.

Can I use balance beads and wheel weights together?

It's best not to. Remove the old weights when you add beads, or the beads will end up fighting the weights.

Can I use balance beads with tire sealant?

No. Sealant and beads clump together and stop doing either job.

Do lawn mower or forklift tires need balancing?

Usually not. They don't go fast enough for imbalance to matter, and solid or foam-filled tires can't be balanced the normal way.
