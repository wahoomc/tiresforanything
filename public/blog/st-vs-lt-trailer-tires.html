Trailer tires vs. car tires: why ST tires exist

Why trailers use ST tires, when LT tires make sense, why car tires are a bad idea, plus speed ratings, load range and the air pressure trailer tires need.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated November 12, 2026About 7 minutes

Trailer tires seem to fail more than just about any other tires on the road. Usually it isn't because they're cheap. It's because they're the wrong tire, the wrong pressure, or just plain old.

Here's the thing most people miss. Trailer tires get abused just by sitting. A trailer parked in the sun for months can have tires that are rotting, and you won't know it by looking at the tread. I've never had a trailer tire blow out on me on the side of the road, but sun and age are exactly how the ones that do blow out get there.

What makes an ST tire different

ST stands for special trailer. Your truck's tires have to steer, brake and put power down. A trailer tire doesn't do any of that. It just has to carry weight and follow along without wagging. So ST tires are built for that job:

  • Stiffer sidewalls to fight sway and keep the trailer tracking straight behind you.
  • Higher load capacity than a car tire of the same size.
  • Tougher construction for sitting loaded in one spot for weeks, which tires hate.

What they give up is ride, grip and handling. That's why ST tires belong on trailers and nowhere else.

Can I put car tires on my trailer?

You shouldn't. A passenger car tire has softer sidewalls and less load capacity, so it lets the trailer sway more. If you ever have to run one, the industry rule is to knock about 10% off its load rating for trailer use. Most of the time that pushes it below what your trailer needs.

What about LT tires?

Light truck tires are a legitimate choice, especially on heavier campers and equipment trailers. They often have higher speed ratings, and some people like the way they handle. Make sure the load capacity matches what your axles carry. On a lighter trailer, an LT tire can ride stiff enough to rattle things loose.

Watch the speed rating

This one catches people. Many ST tires were built for a max of 65 mph, and if there's no speed letter on the sidewall, plan on 65. Newer ones are often rated higher, like L (75 mph) or M (81 mph). Pulling a trailer at 75 on tires built for 65 heats them up, and heat is what kills trailer tires.

Load range in plain English

The load range letter tells you how much the tire can carry at its max pressure. Common trailer ranges are C, D and E, from lighter to heavier. Add up your loaded trailer weight, subtract what sits on the hitch, and make sure your tires together can carry the rest with some room to spare.

Air pressure: the number that matters most

Many trailer tire makers say to run ST tires at the max pressure on the sidewall, cold. Check what your tire's maker recommends. Either way, it's different from your car, where you go by the door sticker. Underinflation is the number one killer of trailer tires. It flexes the sidewall, builds heat, and one day the tread comes off.

Check pressure every trip, before you go, while they're cold. A cheap gauge and a little compressor in the tow vehicle will save you a lot of grief.

Old tires look fine until they aren't

Trailers sit. Tires age even when they don't roll, and sun cracks the sidewalls. A trailer tire with plenty of tread can still be too old to trust. Many makers suggest replacing trailer tires around every 3 to 5 years no matter how they look. Use the DOT age checker on our trailer tire page to find out how old yours are, and our DOT date code guide explains how to read the code. If the trailer lives outside, tire covers are cheap insurance against the sun.

Quick checklist

  • ST or properly rated LT tires, never passenger tires
  • Load range covers your loaded weight with room to spare
  • Speed rating covers how fast you actually tow
  • Pressure set to sidewall max, cold, before every trip
  • Tires less than about 5 years old
  • Spare checked too (it's usually the oldest tire you own)
  • Covers on the tires if the trailer sits outside

Common questions

Can I put LT tires on my trailer?

Yes, if the load rating fits your trailer. Many owners of heavier trailers do.

How fast can I drive on trailer tires?

Check the speed letter on the sidewall. No letter usually means 65 mph.

Why do my trailer tires keep blowing out?

Most often it's low pressure, overloading, speed or age, or some mix of all four.

How long do trailer tires last?

Many tire makers suggest replacing them every 3 to 5 years regardless of tread, because sun and sitting break down the rubber.

Do trailer tires need balancing?

If the trailer runs highway speeds, yes. Our balancing guide covers it.
