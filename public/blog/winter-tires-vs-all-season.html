Winter tires vs. all-season: do Midwest winters need them?

All-season, all-weather or true winter tires? What the snowflake symbol means, when winter tires are worth the money, and how to make switching easier.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotUpdated November 11, 2026About 6 minutes

Every November somebody asks me if they really need winter tires. My honest answer is, it depends on where you drive, when you drive, and who's in the car. Let me lay it out so you can make the call yourself.

The quick answer

Fine where roads get plowed fast and you can stay home on bad days. Grip fades below about 45°F.
All-weather
Year-round tires that earned the snowflake symbol. The sweet spot for a lot of Midwest drivers.
Winter
The best stopping and turning on snow and ice. Worth it for rural roads, early shifts and new teen drivers.

What all-season tires really are

All-season tires are built to be decent at everything: dry roads, rain, a little snow, long tread life. They're the tires on most cars when they leave the factory. They're a compromise, and for a lot of people, a good one.

The problem is the rubber. All-season rubber gets stiff as it gets cold, and a stiff tire doesn't grip well. Once you're consistently below about 45 degrees, an all-season tire starts losing grip, even on dry pavement. Then add snow and ice.

What winter tires do differently

Winter tires are made from a softer rubber that stays flexible in the cold. The tread is deeper, with more grooves and thousands of tiny slits called sipes. Those sipes act like little edges that bite into snow and ice. The tread also packs snow into itself on purpose, because snow grips snow better than rubber grips snow.

The big difference shows up in stopping and turning, not just getting going. All-wheel drive helps you get moving. It does nothing to help you stop. That's the part people forget.

Look for the snowflake

Two markings get confused all the time:

  • M+S (mud and snow) is put on by the tire maker. No test is required. Lots of all-season tires have it.
  • The three-peak mountain snowflake symbol means the tire passed a standardized snow traction test. True winter tires have it. So do all-weather tires.

If the tire only says M+S, don't assume it's a snow tire.

The middle option: all-weather tires

All-weather tires carry the snowflake symbol but are made to stay on the car all year. They're better in snow than an all-season and better in summer heat than a winter tire. They aren't as good on ice as a true winter tire.

For a lot of Midwest drivers who don't want to swap tires twice a year, this is the sweet spot.

So which one do you need?

Have a new teen driverWinter tires, honestly
Drive a rear-wheel-drive car or a pickup with an empty bedWinter tires

Making winter tires easier

  • Buy all four. Two winter tires on one end of the car makes it want to spin around in a slide. Always four.
  • Get a second set of wheels. Steel wheels are cheap. Then swapping is just a tire rotation, and you skip paying for mounting and balancing twice a year.
  • Watch the calendar, not the forecast. A common rule is on around Thanksgiving and off around Easter, once it's staying above about 45 degrees.
  • Store them right. Cool, dry and out of the sun. Stack tires that are on wheels, or stand bare tires up.
  • Check stud rules. If you're thinking about studded tires, your state probably limits when you can run them.

Common questions

Can I just put winter tires on the front?

No. Mismatched grip front to back can spin the car around. Run four.

Do I need winter tires with all-wheel drive?

AWD helps you go. It doesn't help you stop or turn. Tires do that.

Can I leave winter tires on all summer?

You can, but they wear fast in heat and handle worse. You'll burn up an expensive set.

What's the difference between all-season and all-weather?

All-weather tires earned the three-peak mountain snowflake symbol in a snow test. Most all-season tires didn't.

When should I put winter tires on?

When temperatures stay below about 45 degrees. In the Midwest that's often around Thanksgiving, and they come off around Easter.

Need your size first?

Find it on your door jamb label or sidewall, then look it up.
