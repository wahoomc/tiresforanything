How to pick a portable tire inflator for the car, truck, trailer and garage

Cordless, 12V or garage plug-in? What PSI, airflow and duty cycle mean, and which kind of tire inflator fits cars, trucks, trailers, bikes and mowers.

By Brad, former Class 8 tire tech, Army Reserve motor sergeant and private pilotPublished November 30, 2026 at 7:56 p.m. ETAbout 4 minutes

If there's one tire tool I'd put in every vehicle and every garage, this is it. A portable inflator turns a low tire from a gas station trip into a two-minute job, and it makes checking pressure something you'll actually do. It also makes a pretty good Christmas gift for the person in your life who never checks theirs.

One thing I would not do is buy the $8 12-volt air compressor on the bottom shelf in the back of Harbor Freight. It might be OK for a small kid's bike. But by the time it gets a car tire filled, the motor's burned up, and you could have carried the tire on your back, walked into town and found a real air compressor.

I'm not going to rank brands here. Instead, here's how to pick the right type, so whatever you buy fits what you're filling.

The quick answer
Cars and small SUVs
A cordless or 12V inflator with auto shutoff covers it.
Trucks, trailers and RVs
Get one rated well above your highest tire pressure, with more airflow and a longer run time.
Bikes, balls and mowers
Look for low-pressure accuracy and the right valve adapters.
Garage
A plug-in model that also runs on 12V does double duty.

Cordless (battery)Quick top-offs, bikes, mowers, glove boxBattery runs down on big tires; keep it charged in winter
12V (plugs into the vehicle)Truck, trailer, road tripsRun the engine so you don't drain the battery
AC/DC (wall and 12V)Garage plus vehicleBulkier to carry

The numbers on the box

  • Max PSI. Pick one rated well above the highest pressure you need. Trailer and light truck tires can run 65 to 80 psi or more; a car is usually 30 to 40.
  • Airflow (CFM or liters per minute). How fast it fills. Big truck and RV tires need more airflow or you'll be standing there a long time.
  • Duty cycle. How long it can run before it needs to cool down. Cheap units may need a break after 10 minutes, which matters on four big tires.
  • Auto shutoff. Set the pressure and walk away. Worth having.
  • Hose and chuck. A screw-on chuck seals better than a clip-on. A longer hose reaches the far side of a trailer.

Match it to what you're filling

  • Car or SUV: Almost any decent inflator works.
  • Pickup or SUV with LT tires: Check the max PSI covers your load range.
  • Trailer or camper: ST tires often run at the max sidewall pressure, so get extra PSI headroom and a long hose. Our trailer tire guide explains why.
  • Bikes: You'll need a Presta adapter for road bikes. Road tires run high pressure in a tiny volume.
  • Mowers, wheelbarrows, snowblowers: Low pressures, so accuracy at 10 to 20 psi matters more than speed.

Don't trust the built-in gauge blindly

The gauge on a cheap inflator can be off by a few psi. Check it against a good separate gauge the first time you use it. After that you'll know how much to adjust.

Common questions

Can a portable inflator fill a flat tire?

Yes, if the tire is still sealed on the rim. It'll take a while on a big tire. If it won't hold air, it needs a repair.

Can a car inflator do trailer tires?

Only if its max PSI comes in well above what your trailer tires need. Many trailer tires run 65 psi or more.

Cordless or 12V?

Cordless is handier for quick top-offs. 12V never runs out of battery as long as the engine runs.

Will it work in the cold?

Most do, but batteries lose power in the cold. Keep a cordless one charged and inside when you can.
