Reading a forklift tire size

Cushion (press-on)
18x7x12-1/8

Outside diameter 18 in, width 7 in, inside diameter 12 1/8 in. That last number has to match your wheel exactly.

Pneumatic
7.00-12

Section width 7.00 in on a 12 in rim. You'll also see three-part sizes like 18x7-8 (diameter x width - rim).

Not sure? Paste the size into the size decoder.

When to replace

  • Press-on tires worn down to the molded wear line on the sidewall
  • Chunks missing, tearing, or cracks across the tread
  • Flat spots, or the truck rides lower on one side
  • Rubber starting to separate from the steel band
Replace in pairs

Always change both tires on the same axle. Mismatched wear tilts the mast and makes the load less stable.

All tires / Aviation
General aviation & up

Aircraft tires

An airplane tire carries the whole aircraft on landing, often at more than 100 mph. That's why the sizing, ply rating and markings are stricter than anything on the road.

Check the book first

Always confirm the approved size and ply rating in your aircraft's parts catalog or POH. Have the work done and logged the way federal regulations require, by you as owner-pilot where the rules allow it, or by an A&P mechanic. This page helps you understand the options. It doesn't replace your mechanic or your aircraft's records.

Every aircraft tire listed on this site is labeled either TSO / FAA-approved or Not FAA-approved: experimental and light-sport only. A tire that fits a wheel is not necessarily approved for your aircraft.

Three ways aircraft tires are sized

Type III · most piston singles6.00-6

Section width 6.00 in on a 6 in wheel. Low pressure, made for light aircraft.

Three-part · newer designs15x6.0-6

Outside diameter 15 in, width 6.0 in, wheel 6 in. Used on Cirrus, the 172RG and others.

Type VII · jets26x6.6

Outside diameter and width only. High-pressure tires for jets and heavier aircraft.

Ply rating means load, not layers

The ply rating tells you how much weight the tire is certified to carry at its rated pressure. It's not a count of the layers inside. A Cessna 172 and a Cessna 180 both use a 6.00-6 main tire, but the heavier 180 calls for a 6-ply rating while the 172 needs only a 4-ply. A higher rating isn't automatically better. Use what your aircraft calls for.

Options to know

New vs. retread

Retreads put new tread on an inspected casing for a lower price. Plenty of flight schools and owners run them on the mains.

Tube-type vs. tubeless

Many light singles run tube-type tires. Premium low-leak tubes hold pressure longer between top-offs.

Tundra and bush tires

Oversized, low-pressure tires for gravel bars and grass strips. Most need an STC for certificated aircraft.

What's on the sidewall

TSO number, size, ply rating, speed rating in mph, tube or tubeless, serial number, and a red balance dot.

All tires / Passenger cars
Sedans · coupes · minivans · EVs

Passenger car tires

Your car already tells you which tire it needs. Find the size, then pick the type that fits how and where you drive.

Find your size in 30 seconds

Best source

Driver's door jamb

Open the driver's door and look for the tire and loading information label. It lists the original size and the recommended cold tire pressure.

Also works

Owner's manual

The specifications section lists every factory size for your model, including optional wheels.

Quickest

The tire itself

Read the size off the sidewall, like 225/60R17 99H, and paste it into the size decoder.

Shop by vehicle

A year, make and model lookup is coming soon. Until then, the door jamb label and the size decoder will get you the exact size.

Pick the type

All-season

The default on most cars. Good in dry and wet weather and light snow, but not a true winter tire.

All-weather

An all-season that carries the three-peak mountain snowflake symbol, so it's rated for severe snow. A good fit for Indiana-style winters if you don't want to swap tires.

Winter

Soft rubber and deep siping that grip below about 45°F. Best for heavy snow and ice, used only in the cold months.

Summer and performance

Sharp handling and braking in warm weather. Loses grip in the cold and isn't meant for snow.

Touring

A quiet, comfortable all-season built for long tread life.

EV and run-flat

EV tires handle extra weight and instant torque, often with foam for less road noise. Run-flats let you drive a short distance after a puncture.

Load index and speed rating

The two characters at the end of the size, like 99H, are the load index and speed rating. Never go below what your door label calls for. Going higher is fine.

Speed ratingTop sustained speedCommon on
S T112 to 118 mphFamily cars, minivans, touring tires
H130 mphMost newer sedans and crossovers
V149 mphSport sedans and performance trims
W Y168 to 186 mphHigh-performance and sports cars

A load index of 99 means each tire can carry up to 1,709 lb at its rated pressure. The decoder shows the number for any size you enter.

When to replace

  • 2/32 in of tread is the legal limit in most states. A few differ, so check yours.
  • The penny test: put a penny in the tread with Lincoln's head down. If you can see his whole head, you're at about 2/32 in.
  • Wear bars molded across the tread mean the tire is done when they're flush with the surface.
  • Cracks, bulges or uneven wear are reasons to replace, or to get an alignment check, no matter the tread depth.
All-wheel drive?

Many AWD vehicles need all four tires closely matched in size and tread depth. Check your owner's manual before replacing just one or two.

Legal limits from Tire Rack's tread depth guide. California and Idaho use 1/32 in; Texas inspections use 3/32 in.

Light truck and SUV tires

Truck tires come down to two questions: how much weight you carry or tow, and how much of your driving is off the pavement.

Pick the tread

Highway

Quiet, smooth and long-wearing. For trucks that stay on pavement.

All-terrain

The middle ground: good on the road, capable on gravel, dirt and snow. Many carry the snowflake symbol.

Rugged-terrain

Tougher than all-terrain, quieter than mud-terrain. Popular with work trucks.

Mud-terrain

Big open lugs for mud and rocks. Louder and wears faster on the highway.

Going bigger?

  • Taller tires change your speedometer and gearing, and may need a leveling kit or lift to clear.
  • Check for rubbing at full turn and full suspension travel.
  • Keep the load index at or above the factory tire's rating.

ATV, UTV and golf cart tires

Most of these tires ship to your door, and many come pre-mounted on wheels, so you can do the job in the garage. Start with the size, then match the tread to your ground.

Reading the size

ATV, bias
25x8-12

Diameter 25 in, width 8 in, on a 12 in wheel. Front and rear are often different widths, like 25x8-12 up front and 25x10-12 in back.

UTV, radial
27x9R14

The R means radial, which rides smoother and runs cooler at side-by-side speeds.

Golf cart
18x8.50-8

The standard non-lifted golf cart size. Low-profile metric sizes like 205/50-10 are common on custom wheels.

Pick the tread

All-terrain and trail

Medium lugs that handle dirt, gravel and roots. The right pick for most riders.

Mud

Tall, widely spaced lugs that clean out in deep mud. Rough and noisy on hard ground.

Sand

Paddle rears and smooth or ribbed fronts that float on dunes.

Turf and street

Low, gentle tread that won't tear up a golf course or lawn. Street-legal low-speed carts may need tires that meet your state's equipment rules.

Ply rating and lifted carts

  • Ply rating is toughness, not layers. 2-ply rides softest on easy trails. 6- and 8-ply resist punctures on rocks and heavier UTVs.
  • Bigger golf cart tires, like 22 or 23 in, usually need a lift kit to clear the body.
  • Pre-mounted wheel and tire sets bolt straight on. No bead breaker, no balancing.
All tires / RV & motorhome
Class A · Class B · Class C

RV and motorhome tires

RV tires carry a lot of weight and often sit for months between trips. Get the weight right and keep an eye on age, and they'll treat you well.

What each class uses

Class A

Commercial-truck tires

Built on bus or truck chassis, so they run sizes like 275/80R22.5 and 245/70R19.5. Installed by commercial tire shops.

Class C

Light truck tires

Usually LT tires on a cutaway van chassis, often with dual rear wheels. Load range E is common.

Class B

Van tires

LT or van tires sized for the chassis they're built on. Follow the label on the driver's door jamb.

Class A owners: see the semi and commercial guide for 22.5 and 19.5 sizes and commercial dealers.

Weigh it, then set the pressure

  1. Weigh the RV loaded for a trip, at a truck stop scale or an RV weighing event. Corner-by-corner weights are best.
  2. Find the heaviest tire position on each axle.
  3. Look up that weight in the tire maker's load and inflation table, and set every tire on that axle to match.
  4. Check pressure cold, before you drive, every trip.
Age matters as much as tread

RV tires often crack from sun and time before the tread wears out. Check the DOT date code with our tire age checker, and cover the tires when you're parked for long stretches.

Duals and monitoring

  • Match dual tires in size, brand and wear, so one doesn't carry more than its share.
  • Valve extenders make it possible to check and fill the inner dual.
  • A tire pressure monitoring system warns you about a slow leak before it becomes a blowout.

Wheelchair and mobility scooter tires

A flat on a wheelchair or scooter isn't an inconvenience, it's a day stuck at home. Here's how to find the right replacement and avoid the next flat.

Check with your equipment supplier first

If your chair or scooter came through insurance or a medical equipment supplier, ask them before buying tires. Repairs may be covered, and some power chairs need parts approved by the maker.

Reading the size

Manual chair, rear
24x1-3/8

A 24 in wheel with a 1 3/8 in wide tire. The same tire is marked 37-540 in metric.

Front caster
8x1

Casters are measured by diameter and width, usually 5 to 8 in. Note the axle size before ordering.

Scooter
3.00-4

A 3 in wide tire on a 4 in rim, also listed as 260x85. One of the most common scooter sizes.

Size finder

SizeAlso listed asUsed onNotesShop

Always confirm against the size molded on your current tire or in your owner's manual. Sources: Schwalbe wheelchair tires · PU Tire scooter sizes · Scooter tire size guide

Air, solid or flat-free?

Pneumatic

Air-filled. The smoothest, lightest ride, but they can go flat and need regular pressure checks.

Solid

Polyurethane or solid rubber. Never flat and no upkeep, with a firmer ride.

Flat-free inserts

A foam insert inside a pneumatic tire. Keeps much of the cushion without the flats.

Hospital beds, carts and walkers use casters rather than tires. Match the wheel diameter, stem type and load rating.

All tires / Race cars
Autocross · track days · road racing · drag · dirt oval

Race car tires

The right race tire depends on where you run, what your class allows, and how many weekends you need it to last. Start with the rules, then choose grip versus life.

Check your class rules first

Most sanctioning bodies specify approved tires, sizes or a minimum treadwear rating. Many autocross street classes, for example, require a treadwear rating of 200 or higher. A faster tire that isn't legal for your class won't help you.

Drag and dirt

Drag racing

Drag radials work on street cars and many are DOT-marked. Slicks hook harder but are for the strip only. Up front, tall, skinny front-runners cut weight and rolling resistance.

Dirt oval

Track-specific compounds, often grooved or siped by hand for the night's conditions. Your track or series usually names the approved tire.

Getting the most from race tires

  • Set pressure hot, not cold. Check right after a session and adjust toward your target hot pressure.
  • Read the temperatures. A pyrometer reading across the inside, middle and outside of the tread tells you if pressure and camber are right.
  • Mind heat cycles. Each heat-up and cool-down costs a little grip, even with tread left.
  • Competition-only tires stay on the trailer. If the sidewall says "for competition use only," it isn't legal on public roads.
  • Store them cool and dark, in bags, away from electric motors that give off ozone.

Go-kart tires

In karting, the tire often comes down to your class rules. Start with what your sanctioning body allows, then pick the compound for your track and budget. Racing a car instead? See race car tires.

Most racing classes specify approved tires, sizes or treadwear ratings. A tire that's faster but not legal for your class won't do you any good.

Karting

Sprint kart sizes
10x4.50-5

A common front size. Rears are wider, like 11x7.10-5, both on 5 in wheels.

Compounds

Hard to soft

Harder compounds last longer and suit practice and spec classes. Softer compounds grip harder but wear out fast. Rain tires have their own tread and compound.

Farm tires

Farm tires are sorted by the job they do. A short industry code on the sidewall, like R-1 or I-3, tells you what the tread was built for, so start there.

Reading a farm tire size

Bias, inch
18.4-38

Section width 18.4 in on a 38 in rim. The classic rear tractor size.

Metric radial
480/80R46

Width 480 mm, sidewall 80% of the width, on a 46 in rim. Common on row-crop tractors and sprayers.

IF and VF
IF480/80R46

IF tires carry 20% more load than a standard tire at the same pressure. VF tires carry 40% more. Lower pressure means less soil compaction.

Radial or bias?

Radial

Bigger footprint, better traction, less compaction and a smoother ride. Costs more up front.

Bias

Tough sidewalls and a lower price. Still common on utility tractors, older equipment and implements.

  • Set pressure by load, using the tire maker's load and inflation table, not a single number for every job.
  • On front-wheel-assist tractors, front and rear tire sizes have to stay in the ratio your tractor's manual calls for. Mixing sizes can strain the drivetrain.
  • Ballast, whether wheel weights or liquid fill, changes how a tire carries load. Check the tractor manual before adding or removing it.
Service comes to the field

Big farm tires are usually mounted on-site by a dealer's service truck. That's why this category runs on local quotes instead of online checkout.

Semi and commercial truck tires

Truck tires are sold by wheel position and by the kind of miles you run. A steer tire and a drive tire can share a size and still be built for very different jobs.

Pick by position

Front axle

Steer

Rib tread for even wear, stable handling and a smooth ride. The tire that matters most for control.

Driven axles

Drive

Deeper lug tread that puts power to the road in rain, snow and on grades.

Trailer axles

Trailer

Free-rolling tread built to resist scrubbing in tight turns and to carry the load.

All-position tires can run on any axle and are common on medium-duty trucks.

Then pick by service

Long haul

Highway miles at steady speed. Built for low rolling resistance and fuel economy.

Regional

Shorter routes with more turns and stops. Tougher against scrubbing and curb hits.

Urban and delivery

Constant stops, tight corners and curbs. Extra sidewall protection.

On/off-road

Dump, logging and construction trucks. Cut- and chip-resistant tread.

Common sizes

SizeWhere you'll see it
295/75R22.5Class 8 tractors and trailers, low profile
11R22.5Class 8 tractors, trailers and buses
11R24.5 285/75R24.5Class 8 trucks on taller 24.5 in wheels

What the federal rules say

Minimum tread depth

Front tires on a bus, truck or truck tractor need at least 4/32 in of tread in a major groove. All other tires need at least 2/32 in. No bus may run regrooved, recapped or retreaded tires on its front wheels. 49 CFR 393.75

Retreads

A new tread on an inspected casing, at a lower cost than new. Common on drive and trailer positions, and a big part of how fleets manage tire costs.

Matching duals

Dual tires should match closely in size and wear. A mismatch makes one tire carry more than its share of the load.

All tires / Trailers
Utility · boat · camper · horse · car hauler

Trailer tires

Trailer tires are built to carry weight, not to steer or drive. They have their own rating system, and they tend to age out before they wear out.

ST, LT or P?

ST205/75R15

Special Trailer

Stiffer sidewalls to fight sway and carry heavy loads. For trailers only, never a tow vehicle.

LT235/85R16

Light Truck

Sometimes used on heavier trailers. Make sure the load rating covers your trailer's weight.

P205/75R15

Passenger

Built for cars. Generally not a good fit for trailers, and passenger tires lose load capacity when used on one.

Load range

Load rangePly ratingTypical use
C6Light utility and small boat trailers
D8Campers, car haulers and mid-size boats
E10Heavy campers, toy haulers and equipment trailers

The exact weight each tire carries depends on its size and pressure. It's printed on the sidewall.

How old are your trailer tires?

The last four digits of the DOT code on the sidewall are the week and year the tire was made. 2324 means the 23rd week of 2024.

Keep them rolling

  • Check pressure cold, before every trip. Many ST tire makers recommend running at the maximum pressure on the sidewall. Check yours.
  • Know your speed limit. Look for the speed symbol on the sidewall and don't tow faster than it allows.
  • Replace by age, not just tread. Trailers often sit, and sun and time crack rubber long before the tread wears.
  • Match tires on the same axle, and cover them when the trailer is parked for months.

Skid steer tire options

Pneumatic

Air-filled, lowest cost and best ride. Flats are the trade-off on demolition and scrap jobs.

Leave big wheels to the pros

Large OTR tires often mount on multi-piece rims that can separate with deadly force under pressure. OSHA has specific rules for servicing these wheels. Have a trained OTR technician do the work.

Lawn and garden tires

Most mower and small-equipment tires ship right to your door and go on in the garage. Match the size, then pick the tread for the job.

Reading small tire sizes

Three-part
20x10.00-8

Outside diameter 20 in, width 10 in, on an 8 in rim. A common riding mower rear.

Three-part
15x6.00-6

Diameter 15 in, width 6 in, 6 in rim. A common riding mower front.

Dual-section
4.10/3.50-4

Fits as either a 4.10 or 3.50 section on a 4 in rim. Wheelbarrows and hand trucks.

Pick the tread

Turf

Shallow, wide tread that won't tear up grass. The standard for mowing.

Rib

Straight ribs that steer cleanly. Common on front tires.

Lug or bar

Deep cleats for dirt, mud, snow and pulling a cart. Hard on a nice lawn.

Flat-free

Solid polyurethane foam that never goes flat. Popular on wheelbarrows, hand trucks and zero-turn front casters.

  • Check the ply rating if the tire carries real weight, like a loaded cart or a snow blade.
  • Tube or tubeless: many small tires are tubeless but need a good bead seal. A tube is an easy fix for a tire that won't hold air.

Motorcycle tires

Motorcycle tires come in matched front and rear pairs, and each one is built for its position. Start with the sizes your manufacturer lists. Then, if you're curious, read about the alternatives some touring riders choose, and the risks that come with them.

Reading a motorcycle tire size

Metric radial
180/60R16

Width 180 mm, sidewall 60% of the width, radial construction, on a 16 in wheel. Common on newer touring bikes.

Metric bias
150/80-17

Same layout, but the dash means bias-ply construction. Don't mix radial and bias front to rear unless your bike's maker allows it.

  • Front and rear tires are different by design. A front tire is built to steer and brake. A rear tire is built to drive.
  • Follow the rotation arrow molded on the sidewall when the tire is mounted.
  • Match the load and speed ratings listed in your owner's manual or on the bike's tire label.

Alternative fitments

Darkside and double darkside

Some touring and cruiser riders run a car tire on the rear of their motorcycle, a practice called going to the darkside. Tire and motorcycle makers don't approve it, so we cover it on its own site, along with rider reports, sizes and the cautions that go with them.
